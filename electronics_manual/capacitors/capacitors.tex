\textbf{Getting acquainted with capacitance} 

\begin{enumerate}[wide]

\item Identify a range of different capacitors in your kits.  What are the largest and smallest values that you have? How do you read the value of the capacitance on the various types?  Use your DMM to measure a few of their values, noting both the uncertainty in your DMM's measurement and how close the capacitors are to their stated values.  What range of capacitance can your DMM measure?

\item Build the circuit shown below.  Two things to note:
\label{part_rc_circuit}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textit{Be sure to connect the positive and negative ends of the capacitor correctly as shown}.  If you reverse the polarity of the capacitor, it can theoretically blow up in your face and hurt you (unlikely) or at least have poor capacitance and a large leakage current (much more likely).  
\item For the switch, use SPDT switch S9 or S10 on your PB-505, just to the left of te speaker.  Before connecting it to your circuit, look carefully at the the small breadboard block to determine which columns of holes are connected to the switche's \textit{common} terminal, and which columns are connected to each of its \textit{throws}.  Use your DMM to confirm your understanding, and make a diagram in your lab notebook showing how these switches are connected.
\end{itemize}

\item Use your oscilloscope to measure the voltage across the capacitor $V_C$ as you move the switch back and forth.  (You will want your scope to be scanning slowly at about 1 second per division, and you may want to use its \textsl{Roll} feature in the panel area for horizontal control.)  Sketch what you see, noting on your graph where your capacitor is fully charged and where it is fully discharged.   

\item Examine the graphs you have just drawn, and predict where on the graphs the current is a maximum, and where it is a minimum.  Now insert your DMM as a current meter between $R$ and $C$, and test your prediction.  Were you correct?

\item When your capacitor is fully charged, throw the switch to discharge it, and measure the time it takes for $V_C$ to drop to about 1/3 of its original value (that is, from 5~V down to about 1.7~V).  This is called the ``time constant'' $\tau$ for this circuit.\footnote{More precisely, $\tau$ is the time for the voltage to fall to $1/e$ of its original value, where $e \approx 2.71828$.}

\item Make a prediction: if you increase the value of $R$, will the time constant increase or decrease?  Write your prediction first, then test it out.

\item Make another prediction: if you increase the value of $C$, will the time constant increase or decrease?  Again, write your prediction first, then test it out.

\item Now that you have several values of $R$, $C$, and $\tau$, care to hazard a guess about an equation relating those three?

\item You have now seen the behavior of $V_C$ as a function of time for a discharging and charging capacitor.  Can you guess the functional form of $V_C(t)$ in each case?

\textbf{A Square Wave Generator}

The circuit above has been acting as a one-time \textit{delay}, with each change in $V_\text{out}$ happening a fixed time after a change in $V_\text{in}$.  But you can use the same principle to make the basic ``tick-tock'' for a repeating, periodic output.  

\item To see how this would work, try pushing the button and then:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textit{As soon as} you see the logic indicator change to high, immediately take your finger off the button, and
\item \textit{As soon as} you see the logic indicator change to low, immediately push the button again, and repeat.
\end{itemize}
Discounting your reaction time, what should you expect for the period of this repeating pattern?  (Hint: the answer is \textit{not} simply twice your answer from Part~\ref{part_time_from_full_charge}, because here the capacitor is never starting from fully charged or fully discharged.)  Make a prediction and test your result.
