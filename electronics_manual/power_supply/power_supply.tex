\textbf{An Op-amp Voltage Regulator?}

You have now seen the limitation of a Zener diode voltage regulator: for small values of $R_\text{load}$ that draw large output currents, you need to lower $R_\text{lim}$ so that the $V_\text{load}$ never falls below $V_R$ of the diode.  But small values of $R_\text{lim}$ allow larger currents through the diode, which leads to larger ripple voltages. (At some point, larger currents would even damage the diode.)
If only there were a way to allow large currents through $R_\text{load}$ while keeping $I_\text{diode}$ small!

\hspace{0.4in}In fact, all we need is a buffer, and the figure below shows how we would do it.  All current to the load now comes from the output of the op-amp, which means we can use a large $R_\text{lim}$ to keep the diode current $I_D$ very small.  With a very small $I_D$, the voltage across the diode remains nearly constant even as the capacitor charges and discharges, ensuring a steady voltage output from the buffer to $R_\text{load}$. 
 
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{power_supply/op-amp_regulator.pdf}

\textit{No need to build this, but do think about how it would work.}
\end{center}

\hspace{0.4in}The figure above appears to perpetrate a blatant fraud: Our entire goal has been to convert an AC voltage to a smooth and stable DC voltage, and yet the op-amp in our circuit requires both $V_\text{CC}$ and $V_\text{EE}$---so where exactly are \textit{those} DC voltages supposed to come from?  In fact, we can connect $V_\text{CC}$ directly to the capacitor voltage $V_\text{cap}$ because as long as $V_\text{CC}$ remains at least 1 or 2 volts higher than $V_-$ at all times, there is is no requirement for the supply voltages to be perfectly constant.%
\footnote{Realistically, the large ripple voltage at the supply voltage would cause \textit{some} ripple voltage at the output as well.  Some op-amps are better than others at reducing that kind of interference.}
And since the op-amp's inputs and output are always positive, it will also be perfectly happy with $V_\text{EE}$ connected to ground.

\textbf{Voltage Regulator ICs}

Regular op-amps aren't the best tool for this job, in part because of their limited output current.  And besides, the circuit above is starting to look like real work to build. Fortunately, the need for smooth and stable DC voltages is very common, so manufacturers have packaged everything within the dashed rectangle above---including the Zener diode---into single \textit{voltage regulator} chips that are optimized for the task. The most common of these are the 78xx series of regulators, as in the 7805 in the circuit below.

\begin{minipage}{0.7\textwidth}

A corresponding 79xx series (7905, 7912, etc.) provides negative output voltages.  Letters before the number indicate the chip's maker ($L$ for STMicroelectronics, $LM$ for Texas Instruments\slash National Semiconductor,  $MC$ for ON Semiconductor\slash Motorola, etc.).  Letters after the number indicate minor variations in performance or packaging.
Most 78xx and 79xx chips come in the same shape: the TO-220 package\footnotemark\
