\begin{enumerate}[wide]

\item A voltage divider is a simple circuit made out of two resistors where the output voltage $V_{\rm out}$ is a fraction of the input voltage $V_{\rm in}$.  (Both $V_{\rm out}$ and $V_{\rm in}$ are measured with respect to ground, so $V_{\rm out}$ is actually the voltage across $R_2$.)  What is the ratio $V_{\rm out}/V_{\rm in}$ for the voltage divider shown below?  Make a prediction, then build it and test it.

\end{center}

\item Design and build a voltage divider that will take an input of 5~V and give an output of 1~V.  (It's fine to combine multiple resistors from your kits in series or parallel to get whatever equivalent resistance you need.)  What is the power rating of your resistors?  Calculate whether they can handle the power demands of your circuit before turning it on and testing it. \label{part_five_to_one}

\bigskip
\textbf{Trimpots}

Any number of resistors in series can be used as a voltage divider, and as the leftmost picture below suggests, changing the connection point of the output terminal changes the fraction $V_\text{out} / V_\text{in}$.  You can also imagine arranging the resistors in a circle and using a rotating dial to select where the output connects to.  The rightmost picture shows a similar device called a \textit{potentiometer}, constructed with a curved piece of resistive material and a rotating wiper arm that slides along it, creating a continuously variable resistance ratio---perhaps for a volume control or something similar.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics{voltage_dividers/circular_divider.pdf}
\end{center}

\item A trimpot%
\footnote{Short for ``trimming potentiometer,'' which nobody actually says in real life}
is a small, cheap potentiometer that is typically used to make fine adjustments to a circuit to correct for slight discrepancies in values of other components.
Your kit contains several trimpots ($\sim 1$~cm blue squares with white dials) ranging in value from $500\ohm$ to $1\Mohm$ every half decade.  Find your $5\kohm$ trimpot (in the same bin as the $4.7\kohm$ fixed resistors) and place it on your breadboard as shown below.  The diagrams above and below show the wiper arm as the middle of the three contacts (numbered pin 2) and that is \textit{almost} always how they are constructed.  Use your DMM to measure resistances between the three pins as you adjust the dial, to determine whether your $5\kohm$ trimpot follows this convention.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics{voltage_dividers/trimpot_pins.pdf}
\end{center}

\item Bonus challenge: can you crack the secret code to decipher how the markings like ``5 0 2'' and ``1 0 3'' relate to the resistance of each trimpot in your kit?  

\item Use your $5\kohm$ trimpot to replace all of the resistors in the circuit you built in part \ref{part_five_to_one}.  Build it and test it.  What is the full range of output voltages you can get from this circuit as you adjust the trimpot?

\item Put some additional fixed resistors in series with a $1\kohm$ trimpot to build a voltage divider that will use an input voltage of 5 volts and provide a variable output voltage that is adjustable between 1.0 and 2.0 volts.  (That is, those are the two output values when the trimpot is in its two most extreme positions.) Draw a diagram of your circuit in your notebook, using the standard circuit symbol for a trimpot shown to the right.\label{part_trimpot_exact}
}

\item Suppose you want to build a voltage divider with a $V_{\rm out}/V_{\rm in}$ ratio of exactly 0.50000.  If you use only a trimpot, then it can be very sensitive and difficult to set exactly.  On the other hand, if you use only fixed resistors, their values aren't guaranteed to be exact, and you may end up with a ratio of 0.510.  Use the same basic idea as in part~\ref{part_trimpot_exact} to design and build a voltage divider using two fixed 10 k$\Omega$ resistors and a trimpot, so that you can guarantee a ratio of exactly 0.500, provided the two fixed resistors are within their stated tolerances.  What value trimpot should you use?  Choose the smallest trimpot value possible for this.  (What's the downside if the trimpot value is too big?)  


\end{enumerate}
