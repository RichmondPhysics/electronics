\bigskip

In \textit{digital electronics}, the logical values \textit{true} and \textit{false} (or ``1'' and ``0'')  are represented by discrete voltages, often 5~V and 0~V.  The high voltage value can be labeled either $V_{\rm CC}$ or $V_{\rm DD}$, for \textit{digital}.

\medskip
\textbf{Generating and measuring digital signals}%
\begin{enumerate}[wide]%,topsep=0pt]
\item To easily switch an input between logical 1 and 0, your PB-505 has a series of eight switches along the bottom labeled S$_1$ through S$_8$.  They are connected internally to a voltage source: either your $+5$~volts or your variable $+V$, selectable with an additional switch just above S$_1$. (We will use $+5$~volts throughout this lab.) Measure the output voltage of one of the switches as you toggle it on and off to confirm that its output voltage behaves as you expect. 

\item Your PB-505 also has eight ``logic monitors'' on the upper right side, for which a red light indicates a high input (logical 1) and a green light indicates a low input (logical 0).  As with the logic switches, you can select  $+5$~volts or the variable $+V$ for the high voltage value used.  You can also select either \textit{CMOS} or \textit{TTL}, which are two different standards that govern exactly what voltage range should count as high, low, or undetermined.  We'll use TTL. Use the very long wires in your kits to connect one of your indicators to one of your logic switches and confirm that it works as expected.

\textbf{Logic Gates}

Digital ICs for different tasks often come in families that have common properties and are designed to work together. On example is the 7400 (or \textsl{74xx}) series, where the digits \textsl{xx} indicate the chip's specific function: AND gates, OR gates, counters, and many others.  They are often adorned with additional letters like \textsl{74LSxx}, indicating ``\underline{\textbf{L}}ow power'' or ``high \textbf{\underline{S}}peed.''

\textit{Note: Although you now know what an open input does for \textit{this} chip, you should \textbf{never} leave a digital input unconnected. \textbf{Comparators}

Sometimes you want to create a digital signal based on whether one voltage is larger than another.  For example, you may want an alarm to be switched from OFF to ON if some temperature, represented by a  
voltage, rises above some predetermined limit. The drawing to the right shows how to use an LM311 comparator as the basis for such a circuit.  If $V_+<V_-$, then pin 7 gets connected to pin 1 inside the chip\footnotemark, so that $V_{\rm out}=0$.  Otherwise, $V_{\rm out} \approx 5$~volts.
